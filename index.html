Eastern Front Model 1941–42

A month-by-month model of the German–Soviet war. Each month it carries forward men, tanks and aircraft on both sides, the German railheads and supply reach on three axes, and the Soviet production base, and moves the front by a dozen mechanisms. Its constants are set so that the real decisions reproduce the real front and the real losses of both years; the assumptions below then play the scenario. Results are the middle of 600 runs, with the 10th to 90th percentile where it matters.

How the model works
  • State. Each month: German men, tanks and aircraft in the east; Soviet field men, tanks and aircraft; the depth of the front on three axes (Leningrad, Moscow, the south, with the Caucasus as a branch); the railheads and supply reach on each; the Soviet production base.
  • Pace. The spearheads' pace is a calibrated open-season rate times the force ratio (a weighted mix of men, tanks and aircraft) to a small, uncertain power (about 0.3) in open country, where the spearheads moved as supply allowed whatever the odds, and to a large one (about 0.7) in a set-piece attack on a held front (Moscow beyond the Mozhaisk line in 1941; the attack of 1942), where force decides, times a season factor (mud in October, frost from November, the thaw in April), and it cannot go beyond the supply reach plus a short overrun. The reach is the railhead plus what lorries can bridge; the railheads follow the front at a limited rate and lag behind it (less in 1942, when the Dnieper bridges and the Donbas network were in use); lorries manage about a third as much in mud and snow, which is what halts Typhoon in the model; a division brings its own transport, so the levers for lorries and fuel are net of what the extra divisions use.
  • Decisions. After Smolensk the Centre recovers (Yelnya, replacements, stocks) and, if the Kiev diversion is on, sends its panzers south; Typhoon opens at the real date, brought forward by an uncertain share of an earlier Smolensk, and two to four weeks earlier without the diversion, at the cost of a flank to cover; beyond the Mozhaisk line the drive on Moscow is a set-piece attack against the Reserve Front and the Moscow defence zone, whose strength grows with the calendar and with Soviet mobilization; the south's slow start and the Luga Line are calibrated factors. In 1942 the Germans attack Moscow first (if set), then the south; a set-piece attack on a held front moves at a fraction of the open pace, cut further by the Moscow defences, and is spent after one to two months if the city has not fallen. Soviet choices: defence in depth, withdrawing from Kiev, stopping the winter offensive in January, no Kharkov offensive.
  • Pockets. Prisoners follow the Soviet men present, the weighted advance, how far forward the Soviets stand, German sealing power (infantry and aircraft), and the alert's leakage in the first two months.
  • Attrition. Soviet killed and wounded follow German combat power and the intensity of the month, more when the Red Army is attacking (the winter, May 1942, the second half of 1942); German casualties follow Soviet power and intensity. Tanks and aircraft are lost as a share of stock each month, more in 1941 than 1942, plus the tanks left in pockets.
  • Production and men. Soviet output follows the real monthly figures, moved by the difference in territory lost (and Moscow's factories); mobilization follows the real inflow; Lend-Lease adds tanks and aircraft from October 1941. German replacements and arrivals follow the real flow, raised by the output and replacement levers.
  • Reserves. The Stavka's weight between the three axes is a choice (in reality the Kiev group of fronts in 1941, Moscow in 1942). An axis holding more than its real share is harder to attack, most of all by a set-piece attack, and yields smaller pockets; one holding less is easier. Moscow is also the hub of the railways: if it falls, the Stavka's capacity to move reserves between theatres drops to an uncertain half to four-fifths (the Volga bypass lines were fewer and slower), half of the loss made good over a year. Fewer reserves then reach the south, which raises the pressure on Stalingrad and cuts the reserves that can be brought to the Don for the November counter-offensive; the winter counter-offensive of 1941–42, whose reserve armies formed around Moscow, is weakened likewise.
  • Weather. How much of October and November 1941 and of the spring of 1942 is lost to mud is drawn for each run, for reality too, so the real settings now give a spread of outcomes around the calibrated run rather than a single one; the calibrated run is the middle of that spread.
  • The winter. The Soviet counter-offensive regains ground on the Moscow axis in proportion to the strength ratio. Tikhvin is retaken in December if the Soviets are strong enough relative to the real winter; if not, Leningrad has no route across Lake Ladoga and capitulates after two to four months.
  • Stalingrad and Uranus. Once the Volga is reached, pressure on the city accumulates with the force ratio and air power; it falls if the pressure passes the real total, which did not suffice, by a margin. The November counter-offensive breaks through if the Soviet reserve power against the German flank reaches a share of the real ratio, which did suffice.
  • Uncertainty. Each mechanism's strength is drawn from a range that acts only on departures from the real war, so the real settings always return the calibrated real run.

An independent review of this version found and I corrected: the Moscow defences had raised Soviet rather than German losses; the German losses of the Moscow attack were understated; the Centre's panzers were counted in the south and at Moscow at once in September; the Luftwaffe was counted twice at Stalingrad, and pressure went on accruing after November; Blue could open in May or never; one uncertainty range leaked into the real run. Calibration: the real run matches the real dates of Minsk, Smolensk, Vyazma, Kiev, Kharkov, Rostov, Daugavpils, Pskov, Shlisselburg, Tikhvin, Voronezh, the Volga, Maikop and Mozdok within a few days to three weeks; the Dec 1941 and 1942 depths, Soviet and German casualties, prisoners, tank and aircraft losses and strengths within about 12 per cent. Two judgements are not calibrated against anything: how far a 1942 set-piece attack on Moscow would get (set so that the real 1942 German army would succeed about half the time at the default defences), and the threshold at which Moscow falls in 1941 (80 km beyond the real halt). The model does not take Leningrad by assault, and it does not decide; the decisions are levers.
